An Empirical Examination of the Role of Analysts' Information in Managers' Voluntary Disclosures

with Somnath Das

Under review at The Accounting Review

Abstract

This paper examines whether and how analysts' information influences managers' voluntary disclosure. Prior literature using analyst coverage as a proxy for the information provided by analysts documents a crowd-out effect: lower coverage increases voluntary disclosure. However, there is little evidence on whether analyst information crowds in, that is, increases managers' voluntary disclosure. We focus on the component of analyst information unavailable to managers, using the measure of analysts' private information proposed by Barron et al. (1998). As the amount of information unavailable to managers increases, so does voluntary disclosure. The crowd-in effect is stronger for firms with higher demand from analysts or managers and weaker for firms with higher costs of voluntary disclosure. Further investigation suggests that the effect is driven in part by the macroeconomic information in analyst forecasts. Overall, the results suggest that analyst information has both crowd-in and crowd-out effects on voluntary disclosure and that managers learn from analysts' information.

Working Paper

Voluntary Disclosure and Investment Inefficiency

with Somnath Das and Rustam Zufarov

Abstract

This paper examines the effect of voluntary disclosure on corporate investment efficiency. The role of voluntary disclosure in attenuating information asymmetries in capital markets is well documented, but whether a firm's investment choices, and particularly its investment efficiency, are affected by managers' voluntary disclosures is less well understood. We document that voluntary disclosure is negatively associated with under-investment and positively associated with over-investment. Results are consistent across several measures of voluntary disclosure and robust to an extensive set of controls. A staggered difference-in-differences design based on firms' inclusion in the S&P 500 index suggests the results are less likely to be driven by endogeneity. Managers' empire-building and propensity to make risky investments exacerbate the positive association between disclosure and over-investment, while the effect is mitigated for firms that operate in multiple segments, face stronger competition, hold more proprietary information, or employ higher-ability managers.

Working Paper

Do Financial Analysts Complement Blockholder Monitoring?

with Somnath Das

Abstract

We investigate whether the presence of financial analysts facilitates blockholder monitoring. Prior work documents that blockholders and analysts each monitor firm outcomes. Because analysts generate and disseminate relevant information, they may provide blockholders with additional information that enhances their ability to monitor. Consistent with this view, the association between blockholder ownership and firm value is stronger in the presence of analysts. The enhancement is stronger for firms that are smaller, younger, riskier, and subject to higher agency costs of free cash flow. Enhanced monitoring mitigates under-investment, lowers excess CEO compensation, and raises announcement returns in mergers. Overall, the results suggest that analysts facilitate and complement monitoring by blockholders.

Working Paper

Net Zero Coefficients in Accounting Research

with Andrew J. Leone and Siqi Zheng

Abstract

A surprising number of empirical accounting studies report regressions in which two coefficients are economically large, statistically significant, and roughly equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. Searching five leading journals from 1996 through 2025, we find the pattern in nearly 10 percent of articles. Building on Kalnins (2018), we derive the conditions under which these opposite-signed coefficients, which we call net-zero coefficients, are an econometric artifact rather than a substantive finding. In the typical case we study, each regressor is positively correlated with the dependent variable on its own, but the sign of one coefficient flips and the magnitudes of both increase when the two variables are included in the same regression. We show that this occurs when the two regressors are highly correlated with each other and are unequally correlated with the dependent variable, and that the second condition can be created simply by adding a control variable. We extend the framework to interacted regressions and show that net-zero coefficients on a main effect and its interaction can also be generated by features of the data, such as unequal variances across groups, that do not reflect a difference in the underlying relation. We illustrate the framework with both real and simulated data. We supplement our derivations and simulations with several empirical examples. Finally, we propose a five-step diagnostic that researchers can use to identify potential Type I errors and inflated magnitudes when such patterns arise.
